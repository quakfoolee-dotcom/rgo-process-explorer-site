Safety basis — argon doors, tanker bay and the A-800 pit (V339)

Status: Proposed, conceptual. This lists what the layout needs and what is still open. It sets no design values. Every number, alarm set point and code reference below is a placeholder for the project safety study and the code review (BC jurisdiction). Nothing here is an approved design.

1. Why this matters

  • Argon is an asphyxiant. It is colourless, odourless, not toxic and not flammable, and it is heavier than air, so a release collects low and flows along the floor, into pits, sumps and through low openings.
  • Liquid argon is cryogenic. A spill boils off to a large volume of gas (roughly 800 times the liquid volume) and causes cold burns on skin and cold-embrittlement of ordinary steel and polymer parts.
  • Cylinders and the regulated header carry pressure.
  • The layout now has two new wall openings next to the argon yard (V336), a tanker bay with a gate (V334), and a lowered A-800 stack in a 4.5 m pit (V330). Each is a place where gas can enter or collect.

3. Open items by place

3.1 South-wall doors (vehicle and pass)

  1. Gas entry through the doors. Argon released outside (tank vent, relief outlets, tanker boil-off, a hose leak) flows low and could enter through a door at grade. The sill height, the distance from the yard (the vehicle door edge at x 36 is about 17 m west of the bay edge at x 52.6 and about 30 m west of the tank) and the prevailing wind are not assessed. Needs a dispersion and ponding screening for the credible releases.
  2. Oxygen monitoring. Whether fixed oxygen monitors are needed inside and outside the doors, at what height (low, because argon is heavy), the alarm levels and what they trigger. Set points come from the safety study (about 19.5 % oxygen is a commonly used lower limit for safe entry; this is not a project value).
  3. Door control. Normally closed or open, interlock of the vehicle door with an alarm, whether the vehicle door can be a ventilation path, who opens it, and how a forklift driver is warned.
  4. Fire and egress. Fire rating of the wall at the openings, door widths and swing, travel distance from the pass door, and the interface with the existing fire points and emergency stations. Not assessed.
  5. Wall structure. Columns beside the openings, headers and sleeves, and the 6 m door against the column pitch are conceptual.
  6. Traffic control. Priority, signs, mirrors and speed at the crossing X-SPUR and at the door; pedestrian and forklift segregation.

3.2 Tanker gate and bay

  1. Swept path. The route model is a forklift. A bulk tanker (about 12 m) needs its own swept-path check through the 4.9 m gate (reversing or forward) and into the bay.
  2. Stand-off distances from the tanker, the fill coupling and the vent and relief outlets to the building, air intakes, doors and people. Supplier and code review.
  3. Gate and bollards. Gate hardware, locking, who controls it, bollard impact rating and foundations (none rated in the model).
  4. Spill and cold liquid. Drainage and surface in the bay (cold liquid on asphalt or concrete), protection of drains from entering the building, and a spill response.
  5. Pedestrian crossing at the gate. There is no marked crossing where the tanker crosses PW-003; the route note says delivery-supervisor control. A marked or gated crossing may be needed.
  6. Tanker operation. Fill procedure, earthing, hose handling, emergency stop and who is on site.

3.3 A-800 pit (confined space)

  1. Ventilation. Extract and fresh-air ducts are modelled; airflow rate, fan duty and standby, duct sizes and the failure case are not designed. Argon collects on the pit floor, so extraction at floor level is the direction of the model, to be confirmed.
  2. Oxygen monitoring. One monitor is modelled; the number, placement, alarm levels, the output to a local alarm and to the control room, and a check procedure are open.
  3. Entry and rescue. Entry permits, no-entry rules, rescue plan and equipment (retrieval line and tripod, breathing apparatus), and who may enter. The pit is a confined space under the jurisdiction's occupational health and safety rules (BC OHS Regulation, confined spaces part; to be confirmed by the code review). The ladder and gate are modelled; the rest is not.
  4. Slab, sump and drainage. Slab cut, waterproofing, the sump destination and pump, groundwater and the water table are not assessed.
  5. Process interlocks. What the oxygen alarm and ventilation failure do to the A-800 feed (stop feeding, close argon supply, ventilate).
  6. Access during operation. Whether anyone needs to enter the pit during operation at all (the feed lift and rotary valve are designed to be serviced by someone); a maintenance-entry basis is open.

3.4 Argon header and regulator station inside the wall

  1. Release at the regulator and header inside the building: room oxygen monitoring near the A-700 station at x 76 and the header, ventilation, and whether the area is classed as a space where oxygen can fall.
  2. Pressure relief and vent routing to a safe location.

4. Decisions needed from QFL

  1. Doors: are the doors normally closed with an alarm, or open? Who controls the vehicle door?
  2. Stand-offs: which standard and which supplier will fix the stand-off distances?
  3. Pit: is routine entry planned? If not, the pit can be locked and treated as permit-only.
  4. Tanker: which tanker size and delivery frequency to design the gate and the swept path for?
  5. Studies: whether to commission a dispersion and ponding study and a confined-space assessment now or after the FEED review.

5. Not in this document

Quantities (airflow, alarm levels, distances), selection of instruments, door and fire-wall ratings, and any approval. No geometry changed in V339.
